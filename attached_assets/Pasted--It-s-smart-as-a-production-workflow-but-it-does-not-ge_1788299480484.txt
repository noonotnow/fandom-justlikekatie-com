**It’s smart as a production workflow, but it does not get around the rights problem.** Firefly can improve the files; it cannot manufacture permission.

There are at least three separate rights layers:

1. **Your grid**
    - You own your original layout, typography, copy, taxonomy, sequencing, and overall compilation.
2. **The source image**
    - The photographer, studio, broadcaster, magazine, platform, or another publisher may own the copyright.
    - Upscaling creates an edited/derivative version; it does not replace the original owner’s copyright.
    - Crediting the source is good practice, but it is not a license.
3. **The actor’s likeness**
    - Publicity, endorsement, and personality rights vary by country and jurisdiction.
    - Editorial discussion or commentary is generally a stronger position than using the actor’s face to sell unrelated merchandise or imply sponsorship.
    - A Firefly license does not grant likeness rights.

So **“Adobe processed it” is not a legal bridge between “I own the grid” and “I can commercially exploit every photograph inside it.”** Adobe’s permission covers using its service and eligible outputs—not any rights missing from your inputs.

## Where your concept is strongest

Vibe Atlas is more defensible when it behaves like an editorial fandom publication:

- Curated visual criticism and commentary
- Meaningful original captions and Vibe analysis
- Images supporting a specific editorial argument
- Clear source attribution
- No claim that the actor endorses Vibe Atlas
- No selling the individual images as stock, posters, or downloadable photo packs
- No removing watermarks or ownership marks
- Limited image resolution where full-resolution reproduction is unnecessary

It becomes riskier when you:

- Sell cards, prints, wallpapers, or merchandise primarily featuring the photographs
- Put actor images directly into advertisements for membership or unrelated products
- Offer high-resolution image downloads
- Enhance and redistribute magazine photography or fan edits
- Use branding that implies an official partnership
- Remove watermarks, signatures, or credits

A paid membership does not automatically destroy an editorial argument—newspapers and magazines charge—but the product has to genuinely remain editorial rather than becoming a paid image library.

## Enhancement can actually increase the concern

If you upscale a low-resolution third-party image and then provide a beautiful high-resolution download, you may be reproducing more market value from the photograph—not transforming away the underlying rights.

Therefore, I would separate:

- **Internal production enhancement:** reasonable operational step
- **Public compressed grid:** editorial presentation
- **Downloadable high-resolution source cards:** do not offer without permission
- **Commercial merchandise:** license first

## Your granularity problem is real

You should not have to process the whole grid. The studio needs a per-card production stage:

> Approved exact nine → open each card → inspect source and rights status → enhance or keep original → approve derivative → render final grid
> 

Each card should have:

- **View original**
- **Download source for production**
- **Send to enhancement**
- Before/after scrubber
- **Use enhanced**
- **Keep original**
- Source owner and URL
- Rights/provenance status
- “Do not enhance” for watermarked, fan-edited, or unclear material

The enhanced files should be used only to render the final grid—not exposed as nine independent high-resolution downloads.

## Practical recommendation

Don’t buy an Adobe enterprise entitlement because you think it solves likeness licensing. It doesn’t.

Buy or integrate the API **only if it solves the per-image production bottleneck**. Separately, create a rights policy and eventually have an IP/media attorney review the actual Vibe Atlas presentation and monetization model.

For launch, I’d prioritize:

1. Official promotional stills and studio-released publicity images.
2. Sources with explicit editorial or promotional-use terms.
3. Original editorial commentary around every grid.
4. No high-resolution individual-image downloads.
5. No merchandise until the relevant photograph and likeness licensing is understood.
6. A takedown/contact process.
7. A real legal review before scaling paid products.

So your instinct is half right: **the enhancement scrub makes the edition more polished and controllable. It does not make the underlying photographs yours.** The smartest version is editorial curation plus provenance and selective licensing—not trying to transform the rights question away.
