1. Executive Summary
1.1 Platform Mission
Vibe Atlas is a spatially organized, quality-driven content discovery platform for 
music and cultural content. Its defining characteristic is a dynamic, living grid 
surface in which the size, position, and visual weight of each content card is a direct 
function of computed quality — not of paid placement, recency alone, or raw 
popularity. The grid is the product: it communicates editorial judgment at a glance, 
rewards authentic creativity, and provides users with a discovery experience that 
feels curated without being opaque.
The platform serves three constituencies simultaneously. Listeners and fans receive 
a discovery surface that surfaces genuinely excellent work, not merely trending or 
promoted content. Artists and creators receive transparency into how their work is 
ranked and tools to understand — and legitimately improve — their standing. 
Curators and editorial teams receive a first-class set of override and annotation 


===== PAGE 3 =====
tools that let human judgment complement algorithmic classification without 
overriding its integrity.
1.2 The Core Problem
The dominant content discovery paradigm across music streaming, social video, 
and cultural media platforms has converged on engagement optimization: maximize 
clicks, plays, shares, and time-on-platform as proxied signals for content quality. 
This paradigm contains a structural flaw: engagement metrics are gameable. 
Velocity-injected plays, bot-driven saves, coordinated cross-platform promotion, and 
paid follower networks all produce engagement signals that are indistinguishable 
from organic discovery at the raw data layer. Over time, platforms optimizing for 
these signals amplify inauthentic content, train their recommendation models on 
corrupted data, and erode user trust in the discovery experience.
The secondary problem is opacity. Creators whose content is demoted or 
suppressed have no mechanism to understand why, no recourse to contest 
erroneous classification, and no signal about what authentic improvement looks 
like. This asymmetry creates antagonism between creators and platforms that 
serves neither party.
1.3 The Architectural Bet
Vibe Atlas makes three architectural commitments that differentiate it from 
engagement-first platforms:
1. Compute quality holistically at index time. Rather than ranking content 
reactively at query time using raw engagement signals, Vibe Atlas computes 
a composite Quality Score for every piece of content on a continuous basis. 
The score integrates engagement, retention, authenticity, and freshness — 
four dimensions that are harder to game in concert than any single metric. 
The score is the first-class indexing artifact; raw engagement data is an input, 
not the output.
2. Make the grid a living surface driven by real-time signals. The 
discovery grid is not a static ranked list. Card dimensions, visual emphasis, 
and grid position update as quality scores change. A track experiencing a 


===== PAGE 4 =====
genuine resurgence physically grows on the grid. A content item flagged for 
inauthentic promotion shrinks or disappears. The grid surface is the visual 
expression of the scoring system, not a layer applied on top of it.
3. Give creators transparency into how their content is ranked. Through 
the Connect Hub integration, creators receive structured score explanations, 
sub-score breakdowns, flag notifications with evidence, and a formal appeal 
pathway. Transparency is a first-class product feature, not an afterthought.

2.2 Data Flow Narrative
The lifecycle of a piece of content — from initial publish to appearance in the 
discovery grid — follows a deterministic pipeline with defined latency budgets at 
each stage.
Step 1 — Ingestion: When a user action (play, save, share, comment) or a 
platform event (content publish, DMCA notice) occurs, the event is published to the 
appropriate Kafka topic. Topics are partitioned by content_id to ensure all signals 
for a given item are consumed by the same downstream consumer group, 
preserving ordering guarantees needed for deduplication. The Signal Normalizer 
consumes events, applies deduplication based on event fingerprints (user × content 
× event_type × 5-minute window), normalizes signal types to a canonical schema, 
and runs a lightweight bot pre-filter (IP velocity, user-agent heuristics) before 
forwarding to sub-score calculators.
Step 2 — Scoring: Sub-score calculators maintain running aggregates per content 
item in Redis. On each signal arrival, the relevant sub-score (Engagement, 
Retention) is recomputed and the composite Quality Score is updated. The full re-
score including Authenticity and Freshness components runs on a 6-hour batch 
cycle. Scores are written to both Redis (for low-latency reads by the Grid Classifier) 
and Postgres (for durable history, audit logs, and the Connect Hub webhook trigger).
Step 3 — Classification: The Grid Classifier reads the updated composite score 
from Redis, applies the classification algorithm (§3.2), and determines the content 


===== PAGE 7 =====
item's grid class. If the class has changed from the previous assignment, a re-
classification event is published, triggering a grid repack for any grid instance that 
includes the item. The classifier also notifies the Search Ranker to reindex the 
item's quality feature vector.
Step 4 — Serving: The UX Rendering Layer assembles grid pages from pre-
computed grid layouts stored in Redis, applying real-time Height Emphasis factors 
(§8) and personalization overlays before response serialization. Search results are 
assembled by the Search Ranker at query time using the four-stage pipeline 
described in §7.
Step 5 — Connect Hub Notification: When a score update exceeds the delta 
threshold (configurable per creator tier), or when a promo flag is raised, the 
Connect Hub bridge emits a webhook to the creator's registered endpoint and 
updates the creator dashboard data store. Appeals and metadata updates flow in 
the opposite direction: Connect Hub → Atlas ingestion pipeline.

3. Grid Classification System

space it occupies on the discovery surface. Classification is not merely a UI decision; 
it is the primary feedback mechanism by which the quality scoring system 
communicates its judgments to users. The grid is the score made visible.

Design Decision: GHOST vs. Removal
GHOST class deliberately preserves content in the index rather than deleting it. This is 
intentional: removal is irreversible and removes the evidence trail needed for appeals. 
GHOST items remain discoverable via direct title search so that creators can verify the 
item still exists and initiate an appeal via Connect Hub. Removal from the index requires 
a separate explicit action (e.g., DMCA takedown confirmed by legal review) and is 
outside the scope of the classification system.

3.3 The Grid Packing Algorithm
Once content items have been classified, the grid packer assembles them into a 
two-dimensional grid layout subject to anti-clustering constraints. The algorithm is a 
modified bin-packing approach that treats the grid as a column-major matrix of unit 
cells.


===== PAGE 12 =====
Anti-Clustering Rules
●
No two adjacent HERO cards: HERO cards (4×4) must be separated by at 
least one column of non-HERO content. The packer enforces this by marking 
a 1-column exclusion zone to the right of each placed HERO card.
●
No column with more than 3 consecutive COMPACT cards: The packer 
tracks per-column consecutive COMPACT counts. When the count would 
reach 4, a STANDARD or higher card is injected from the pending queue.
●
FEATURE card distribution: No more than 2 FEATURE cards may appear in 
adjacent columns at the same vertical position, preventing the grid from 
feeling visually monotonous at mid-tier density.
●
GHOST cards: Not placed in the grid. The packer's input queue filters 
GHOST items before layout computation begins.

3.5 Editorial Pin and Override System
Curators with appropriate role permissions may create time-boxed editorial 
overrides that assign a specific grid class to a content item, bypassing the organic 
classification algorithm. Overrides are not permanent: they carry an expiry 
timestamp, and on expiry the item reverts to organic classification without manual 
intervention.
Key constraints on editorial overrides:
●
Time-boxing: All overrides must specify a duration (minimum 1 hour, 
maximum 30 days). Overrides with no expiry are rejected by the system.
●
HERO approval gate: Elevating content to HERO class via editorial pin 
requires secondary approval from a Senior Curator. The system enforces this 
via an approval workflow in the Admin Console; un-approved HERO pins 
default to FEATURE.


===== PAGE 14 =====
●
Suppression protection: Editorial overrides cannot lift content out of 
GHOST class if the GHOST classification was triggered by an active DMCA 
hold or confirmed promo violation. Legal review must clear the flag before 
editorial tools can act.
●
Audit log: Every override creation, modification, and expiry is written to an 
immutable audit log in Postgres with curator identity, timestamp, and 
justification note.
●
Sponsored labeling: Any editorial pin associated with a commercial 
arrangement (paid placement) must include a sponsored=true flag. This flag 
triggers the "Sponsored" badge in the UX and is enforced at the API level — it 
cannot be omitted by client applications.
3.6 Re-Classification Triggers and Async Worker 
SLA
Re-classification is triggered by any of the following events:
●
Composite Quality Score delta exceeds ± 5 points from the score recorded at 
last classification
●
Promo Detection score exceeds any threshold boundary (see §6.4)
●
Editorial override created, modified, or expired
●
Active flag status change (DMCA hold applied or lifted; community report 
threshold crossed)
●
Forced re-classification requested via Admin Console
The async worker pool processing re-classification events must sustain a P95 end-
to-end latency of ≤ 90 seconds from trigger event publication on the Kafka topic 
to updated class value written to Redis. This SLA is monitored by a Prometheus 
alerting rule; breach of P99 ≤ 3 minutes triggers an on-call page.

4. Quality Score Engine
The Quality Score Engine is the computational heart of Vibe Atlas. It transforms raw 
engagement, behavioral, and contextual signals into a single composite score — a 
float in the range [0, 100] — that serves as the primary ranking artifact across the 
grid classification system, search ranker, and Connect Hub transparency tools. The 
engine is designed to be difficult to game: because the score integrates four 
conceptually distinct dimensions, optimizing for one at the expense of others is 
structurally visible as a signal anomaly.
4.1 Composite Quality Score Formula
The composite Quality Score Q is a weighted linear combination of four sub-scores, 
each independently normalized to [0, 100]:
Q = (0.35 × E) + (0.25 × R) + (0.20 × A) + (0.20 × F)  Where:   E = 
Engagement Score     [0, 100]  — weight: 35%   R = Retention Score      [0, 
100]  — weight: 25%   A = Authenticity Score   [0, 100]  — weight: 20%   F = 
Freshness Score      [0, 100]  — weight: 20%
Heuristic adjustments (§5) are additive post-aggregation, subject to band ceiling 
protection and cap constraints defined in §5.3. The raw Q value before heuristic 
adjustment is stored separately to allow full auditability of the adjustment 
contribution.

content item, normalized against peer content in the same category and release

distribution of the same signals for content in the same category and release cohort 
(content published within the same 90-day window). This ensures that a track 
receiving 1,000 saves in a niche genre competes fairly against a track receiving 
100,000 saves in a mainstream genre.
4.2.2 Retention Score (R) — Weight: 25%
The Retention Score measures whether users who encounter content are compelled 
to stay with it and return to it. It is composed of three components:
●
Completion Rate (50% of R): The median percentage of track/content 
duration consumed per qualified play session. Bucketed into deciles and 
scored 0–100. A completion rate at the 90th percentile of the peer cohort 
scores 90+.
●
Re-engagement Rate (30% of R): The proportion of users who play or 
save the same content item more than once within a 30-day window. High re-
engagement is a strong proxy for genuine quality independent of initial 
discovery context.


===== PAGE 17 =====
●
Session Depth (20% of R): The average number of additional content 
items consumed by users in the same session after encountering this item. 
Content that deepens session engagement — rather than ending it — 
receives a positive signal.
4.2.3 Authenticity Score (A) — Weight: 20%
The Authenticity Score quantifies the probability that a content item's engagement 
signals are organic. It is the primary defense against score inflation via inauthentic 
promotion. A low Authenticity Score is a strong suppressive force on the composite 
Q, regardless of raw engagement volume.
●
Bot Probability (inverse, 40% of A): A_bot = 1 - P(bot | 
engagement_pattern), derived from the Promo Detection ensemble classifier 
(§6). High bot probability directly reduces A.
●
Source Diversity Index (30% of A): Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) 
applied to the geographic, platform-referral, and user-demographic 
distribution of signal sources. Organic discovery produces diverse, 
geographically distributed signals; coordinated promotion produces 
concentrated ones.
●
Velocity Anomaly Score (inverse, 20% of A): Flags sudden, unsustained 
engagement spikes inconsistent with the item's historical trajectory. A 
content item accumulating its entire 30-day engagement in a 6-hour window 
receives a high velocity anomaly score, which suppresses A.
●
Promo Detection Confidence (inverse, 10% of A): Direct feed from the 
Promo Detection service output probability. Values above 0.50 linearly 
suppress A; values above 0.85 zero out A entirely (triggering GHOST 
classification).
4.2.4 Freshness Score (F) — Weight: 20%
The Freshness Score rewards recency while penalizing staleness, using category-
tuned exponential decay to prevent older high-quality content from monopolizing 
the grid indefinitely.


===== PAGE 18 =====
F_base = 100 × e^(-λ × age_in_days)  Category-specific decay constants (λ): 
Viral / trending content:   λ = 0.15  (half-life ≈ 4.6 days)   New releases 
(pop/hip-hop): λ = 0.07  (half-life ≈ 9.9 days)   Album-cycle content: 
λ = 0.03  (half-life ≈ 23 days)   Evergreen / catalog:        λ = 0.01  
(half-life ≈ 69 days)   Archived / legacy:          λ = 0.005 (half-life ≈ 
139 days)
Resurgence Boost Mechanism: When a content item's engagement velocity in 
the trailing 7 days exceeds 2× its trailing 30-day baseline, the Freshness Score 
receives a resurgence boost: F = min(F_base × 1.40, 100). This mechanism 
ensures that genuinely rediscovered catalog content can re-enter competitive grid 
positions without requiring a new release.

5. Quality Heuristics
5.1 Why Heuristics Exist Alongside the Statistical 
Score
The composite Quality Score is a powerful and auditable ranking signal, but it has 
two structural blind spots that heuristics are designed to address.


===== PAGE 21 =====
The Cold Start Problem: Newly published content has no engagement history. A 
purely data-driven score for a brand-new release from a verified, established artist 
would be identical to a score for a brand-new release from an unknown creator with 
no prior activity — both would receive near-zero scores and land in COMPACT or 
GHOST class regardless of intrinsic quality. Heuristics allow the system to make 
reasonable prior assumptions based on creator reputation, content metadata, and 
external signals that exist before organic data accumulates.
Edge Cases the Data Cannot See: Real-world context matters in ways that 
engagement signals cannot capture automatically. A track nominated for a Grammy 
Award, a song that scores on a Billboard chart, or a creator who has been the victim 
of a coordinated harassment campaign leading to artificially elevated community 
reports — these are factual contexts that the statistical model cannot infer from 
engagement data alone. Heuristics provide a structured, auditable mechanism for 
injecting these real-world facts into the ranking system without circumventing the 
scoring architecture.
Design Decision: Heuristics as Bounded Adjustments
Heuristics are deliberately constrained: they cannot override band boundaries for 
suppression decisions, cannot elevate content to HERO class on their own, and are 
subject to a total bonus cap and penalty cap. This prevents heuristic abuse — whether 
accidental or adversarial — from producing outcomes inconsistent with the statistical 
quality signal. A heuristic that would push content into HERO territory is capped at 
FEATURE; a heuristic penalty cannot exceed the Suppressed band floor unless it is itself 
a flag-based suppression (which is handled by the penalty system, not the heuristic 
system).

5.3 Application Rules
●
Total bonus cap: +30. The sum of all positive heuristic adjustments applied 
to a single content item at any given time is capped at +30 points. 
Adjustments beyond this cap are accumulated in a queue and applied as prior 
adjustments expire, up to the cap.
●
Total penalty cap: −50. The sum of all negative heuristic adjustments 
(excluding flag-based GHOST penalties) is capped at −50 points. Content 
cannot be pushed below Q = 0 via heuristics alone.
●
Band ceiling protection: Heuristic adjustments cannot push content into 
the HERO band (Q ≥ 85). The effective maximum for heuristic-adjusted 
content is Q = 84 (top of Gold band). HERO status requires organic scoring at 
the Platinum level.
●
Expiry and recomputation: All heuristic adjustments carry explicit expiry 
timestamps. An expiry worker scans active adjustments every 5 minutes and 
removes expired rules; the Weighted Aggregator triggers a re-score for 
affected content items within the next batch cycle.


===== PAGE 25 =====
●
Heuristic override policy: Heuristics are configured in a versioned Config 
Service, not hardcoded. Changes to heuristic weights or conditions require a 
config change review by the Quality Council (see §5.4) and are logged in the 
heuristic audit trail.
5.4 The Quarterly Quality Council Review
The Quality Council is a cross-functional governance body responsible for reviewing 
and approving changes to the heuristic system, the penalty table, and the sub-score 
weighting parameters. It convenes quarterly and produces a written decision record 
that is stored in the internal engineering wiki and linked from the Config Service 
changelog.
Membership: Head of Platform Engineering, Head of Trust and Safety, Head of 
Creator Relations, Head of Editorial, and one rotating external advisor (music 
industry or academic). Decisions require majority vote; the Head of Trust and Safety 
holds veto rights over any change to the Authenticity sub-score or penalty table.
6. Promo Detection
Promo Detection is the system responsible for identifying and acting on inauthentic 
promotional activity — the practice of artificially inflating a content item's 
engagement signals to improve its Quality Score and grid classification. It operates 
as an independent ML service with its own data pipeline, model governance 
process, and human review workflow.
6.1 Threat Model
Inauthentic promotion takes several structurally distinct forms, each with different 
signal signatures:
●
Velocity bursts: A large volume of engagement signals (plays, saves, likes) 
arriving in a compressed time window, inconsistent with the creator's 
historical trajectory or the content's genre/audience size.


===== PAGE 26 =====
●
Geographic clustering: Engagement signals concentrated in an 
anomalously narrow geographic region, particularly if inconsistent with the 
creator's known fanbase geography or the platform's typical engagement 
distribution for the genre.
●
Bot fingerprints: Device characteristics, IP address patterns, user-agent 
strings, and behavioral timing patterns (e.g., play events with identical 
duration down to the millisecond) consistent with automated script execution.
●
Coordinated account networks: Clusters of accounts that exhibit 
correlated behavior — creating accounts at similar times, following identical 
patterns of playlist addition, or engaging with exactly the same set of content 
items across multiple sessions.
●
Cross-platform synchrony: Engagement spikes that are temporally 
correlated across multiple platforms simultaneously, suggesting coordinated 
off-platform promotion through paid engagement services.
6.2 Signal Groups
Promo Detection signals are organized into three groups, each contributing a sub-
score to the ensemble classifier:
Group A: Engagement Patterns
●
Play velocity (plays per hour, normalized by creator tier)
●
Save velocity (saves per hour, normalized)
●
Play duration distribution (variance from expected distribution; bot plays 
cluster at fixed durations)
●
Engagement-to-impression ratio anomaly (saves per unique impression; 
abnormally high ratios are suspicious)
●
Re-engagement rate spike (sudden large increase in re-engagement 
inconsistent with content age)


===== PAGE 27 =====
Group B: Source Attribution
●
Geographic concentration (HHI of engagement by country; values above 0.80 
are anomalous)
●
Platform referral source distribution (proportion of engagement arriving via 
direct link vs. organic discovery; unusually high direct-link proportion is a 
signal)
●
IP address entropy (Shannon entropy of IP address range diversity; low 
entropy = few source IPs)
●
Known promo service IP ranges (direct match against maintained blocklist; 
deterministic signal)
●
VPN/proxy concentration (proportion of sessions using anonymizing proxies 
above genre-specific baseline)
Group C: Network and Metadata
●
Account age distribution of engaging users (accounts created within 30 days 
of engagement event; high concentration is suspicious)
●
Correlated behavior clustering (graph-based detection of account clusters 
engaging with identical content sequences)
●
Cross-platform timing correlation (temporal cross-correlation with external 
platform signal feeds, where available via API)
●
Creator declaration absence (creator has not submitted a Promotional 
Campaign Declaration via Connect Hub for content with high-velocity 
engagement)
●
Prior promo detection history (creator or associated accounts have previous 
confirmed promo violations)
6.3 The Ensemble Classifier
The promo detection model is an ensemble classifier that combines Group A, B, and 
C sub-scores using a weighted sigmoid function. The architecture is intentionally 
conservative: a bias term is included to reduce false positives, accepting a slightly 


===== PAGE 28 =====
higher false-negative rate in preference to wrongly flagging legitimate organic 
promotion.
# Sub-score computation (each group produces a score in [0, 1]) score_A = 
group_A_model.predict(engagement_features)   # gradient boosted tree score_B 
= group_B_model.predict(source_features)       # logistic regression score_C 
= group_C_model.predict(network_features)      # graph neural network  # 
Weighted combination raw_score = (0.45 × score_A) + (0.35 × score_B) + (0.20 
× score_C)  # Weighted sigmoid with bias term (bias shifts decision boundary 
right, # reducing false positives at the cost of slightly higher false 
negatives) promo_score = sigmoid(raw_score, weight=6.0, bias=-0.8)  # Where: 
sigmoid(x, w, b) = 1 / (1 + e^(-w×(x + b)))

6.5 Human Review Workflow
Any content item that reaches the 0.75–1.00 threshold enters the human review 
queue. The workflow has defined SLAs at each step:
4. Auto-hold (≤ 2 minutes): Content automatically transitions to GHOST or 
STANDARD-cap state (depending on threshold band). A review case is created 
in the moderation platform, populated with the evidence package: promo score 
breakdown, signal group sub-scores, engagement timeline visualization, and a 
comparison to peer cohort distributions.
5. Reviewer assigned (≤ 4 business hours): A trained Trust and Safety reviewer 
is assigned to the case based on workload balancing. The reviewer receives an 
evidence package PDF (auto-generated from the case data) and access to the 
raw signal explorer tool.
6. Decision (≤ 48 business hours from assignment): Three decision paths are 
available:
○
Confirm violation: GHOST or penalty maintained; promo violation 
recorded on creator account; creator notified with full evidence.
○
Clear — organic: Content restored to organic classification; score 
recalculated without promo penalties; creator notified of clearance; 
monitoring flag remains active for 30 days.
○
Clear — declared promotion: Creator had submitted or now submits a 
valid Promotional Campaign Declaration; content restored with 
"Sponsored" badge applied to grid card.
6.6 Creator Appeal Rights
Creators who believe a promo flag is erroneous have a formal appeal right. Key 
parameters of the appeal process:
●
Appeal window: 30 days from the date of the promo flag notification.


===== PAGE 30 =====
●
Appeal submission: Via the Connect Hub 4-step Appeal Flow (§10.7). 
Creators may submit evidence of organic promotion activity: campaign 
receipts, social media posts, press coverage links, distribution records.
●
Appeal review SLA: Appeals are reviewed by a senior Trust and Safety 
reviewer within 5 business days.
●
Appeal outcome: Full reversal (all penalties removed; creator reputation 
heuristic restored), partial reversal (some penalties reduced; monitoring 
maintained), or upheld (original decision stands; creator informed of reason).
●
Escalation path: If a creator believes their appeal was wrongly denied, a 
second-level review by the Head of Trust and Safety is available within 14 
days of the first appeal decision. Second-level decisions are final.

7.2 Stage 1: Query Understanding Service (QUS)
The Query Understanding Service is a Python gRPC microservice that transforms a 
raw user query string into a structured intent object consumed by the retrieval 
stage. It runs on every search request and must complete within 30ms to preserve 
the total P99 latency budget.
QUS Processing Pipeline:


===== PAGE 32 =====
7. Typo Correction: Edit-distance-based candidate generation against a 
phonetic vocabulary index. Corrections are applied silently if confidence ≥ 
0.90; otherwise the corrected query is surfaced as a "Did you mean?" 
suggestion.
8. Intent Classification: A fine-tuned BERT classifier assigns the query to one 
of: Entity Lookup, Mood/Vibe Discovery, Genre Exploration, Chart/Trending, or 
Exploratory. Intent class determines which retrieval path is weighted most 
heavily.
9. Entity Extraction: Named entity recognition identifies artist names, track 
titles, album names, genre terms, and location references. Recognized 
entities are linked to the content knowledge graph to resolve ambiguity (e.g., 
"drake" → artist entity, not the name).
10.Mood and Vibe Tagging: Adjective and descriptor terms in the query are 
mapped to Vibe Taxonomy nodes (§7.6) using a fine-tuned classification 
model. Multi-label output; up to 5 vibe tags assigned per query.
11.Query Expansion: Synonym expansion and related-term injection using a 
music-domain-specific word embedding model. Expansion terms are added to 
the retrieval query at reduced weight (0.3× of primary term weight).
12.Embedding Generation: The full query (original + expanded terms) is 
encoded by the same 768-dim BERT model used to embed content items, 
producing a query vector for FAISS retrieval.

7.3 Stage 2: Retrieval
The retrieval stage runs two parallel paths — lexical and semantic — and merges 
their results using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) before passing the merged 
candidate set to the ranker.


===== PAGE 33 =====
Lexical Path: BM25+ on Elasticsearch
Elasticsearch is queried with a multi-field BM25+ query across indexed fields: title 
(boost 3.0), artist_name (boost 2.5), album_name (boost 1.5), genre_tags (boost 1.2), 
description (boost 1.0). Entity-linked queries use a term filter on entity_id for exact 
entity matching at maximum precision. The lexical path returns the top 200 
candidates.
Semantic Path: FAISS + 768-dim BERT Embeddings
Content items are indexed at publish time with a 768-dimensional BERT embedding 
computed from a concatenation of title, artist name, genre tags, vibe tags, and 
description text. The query vector from QUS is used to perform an IVF-PQ 
approximate nearest-neighbor search in FAISS, returning the top 200 semantic 
candidates. For Similarity mode queries, the seed item's stored embedding vector is 
used directly as the query vector.

8. Height Emphasis System
The Height Emphasis System is a dynamic vertical scaling mechanism that 
modulates the actual rendered height of grid cards beyond their base class 
multiplier. While grid class determines the nominal footprint of a content item on 
the grid, Height Emphasis makes the grid feel alive and editorial by allowing cards 
within the same class to have meaningfully different vertical presences based on 
real-time signals.
8.1 Purpose and Rationale
A grid in which all STANDARD cards are identical in size is visually static. It 
communicates only the binary fact of class assignment, not the richness of the 
signal underneath. Height Emphasis translates the momentum, editorial 
importance, media richness, and personalization relevance of a content item into a 
continuous visual dimension — the vertical axis — creating a surface that users can 
read intuitively without knowing the underlying mechanics.
Design Decision: Continuous vs. Discrete Scaling
An earlier design used discrete height tiers (small/medium/large within each class) 
rather than a continuous HeightEmphasisFactor. Discrete tiers were rejected because 
they produced perceptible "jumps" in card height during re-classification events, and 
because the tier boundaries introduced additional classification parameters that were 
difficult to govern. Continuous scaling within defined clip bounds [0.80, 1.50] produces 
smoother transitions and is governed by a single formula rather than a lookup table.

8.6 Grid Reflow Strategy
Height changes in one card may necessitate reflow of neighboring cards. The reflow 
strategy balances visual stability against responsiveness to score updates:
●
Deferred reflow on scroll intersection: If a card whose height has 
changed is not currently in the viewport, the height update is applied 
immediately to the data model but the DOM reflow is deferred until the card 
enters the viewport intersection observer's threshold (10% visibility). This 
prevents off-screen layout thrashing from impacting scrolling performance.
●
Immediate reflow for small deltas: If the height change is ≤ 1 row unit 
(120px), the reflow is applied immediately regardless of viewport position. 
Small adjustments have negligible impact on layout stability.
●
Reflow batching: Multiple height updates arriving within a 500ms window 
are batched into a single reflow pass to prevent multiple consecutive layout 
recalculations.
●
Scroll position preservation: If a reflow event would shift the user's 
current scroll position, the scroll position is adjusted by the same pixel delta 
to keep the currently centered content in view. This is critical for maintaining 
a non-disruptive reading experience during live grid updates.

9.4 Authentication Model
●
OAuth 2.0 Client Credentials: Connect Hub backend-to-Atlas API 
communication uses OAuth 2.0 client credentials flow. Tokens are issued with 
1-hour TTL; refresh is handled transparently by the Connect Hub SDK.
●
JWT with Creator/Label Scopes: All API requests carry a JWT with scopes 
defined per action. Creator accounts may hold scopes in the creator: 
namespace. Label accounts additionally hold scopes in the label: namespace 
that permit cross-roster actions. Scopes are validated at the API gateway 
before routing to downstream services.
●
HMAC-SHA256 Webhook Verification: All outbound webhooks from Atlas 
to Connect Hub creator endpoints include an X-Vibe-Signature header 
containing the HMAC-SHA256 signature of the request body computed with a 
per-creator shared secret. Creators are responsible for verifying this 
signature before processing webhook payloads. The Atlas documentation 
provides reference implementations in Python, JavaScript, and Go.
●
Webhook retry policy: Failed webhook deliveries (HTTP 4xx/5xx or timeout 
after 10 seconds) are retried with exponential backoff: 30s, 2m, 10m, 30m, 
2h. After 5 failed attempts, the webhook is marked as failed and the payload 
is written to the creator's Connect Hub inbox as a fallback notification.

10. UX Specification
10.1 Design Principles
The UX of Vibe Atlas is governed by three principles that must be held in balance 
simultaneously. These principles occasionally tension against each other; where 
they do, the resolution hierarchy is as listed below.
13.Discovery feels effortless. The grid is the primary product. Users should be 
able to navigate it without reading instructions, understanding scoring, or 
making explicit choices. The quality and classification system communicates 
through visual hierarchy, not through labels. A user who has never heard of 
Quality Scores should be able to accurately intuit that HERO cards are the 
most important items on the page.
14.Context is visible but not intrusive. Users who want to understand why a 
piece of content is prominently placed should be able to access that 
information — but it should not interrupt the discovery flow for users who do 
not. Explainability is accessible via a deliberate interaction (opening the "Why 
this?" drawer), not surfaced unprompted in the main grid view.
15.Creators feel respected. The platform's quality judgment is visible to 
creators through Connect Hub, not just to users. Creators should never be 
surprised by a classification decision without a clear, accessible explanation. 
The UX of Connect Hub creator tools should match the quality of the 
consumer-facing product.

12. Open Questions and Future Work
The following areas represent deliberate decisions to defer architectural design 
beyond the 52-week delivery scope. Each represents a meaningful extension of the 
platform's capabilities that requires either significant additional infrastructure 
investment, resolution of unsettled technical questions, or dependencies on 
external platform decisions.
12.1 Federated Quality Scoring Across Platform 
Partners
The current Quality Score Engine is a closed system: all signals originate on the 
Vibe Atlas platform. A federated scoring model would allow partner platforms — 
distributors, other streaming services, live ticketing platforms — to contribute 
verified, privacy-preserving signal contributions to a content item's Quality Score. 
The primary open question is the trust model: how do we verify the integrity of a 
signal contribution from an external party without exposing raw user data, and what 
prevents a partner from injecting artificially elevated signals?
Candidate approaches include differential privacy mechanisms for signal 
aggregation, zero-knowledge proofs of engagement attestation, or a tiered trust 
model where partner signals are heavily discounted and subject to the same Promo 
Detection pipeline as first-party signals. This work requires both a technical proof-of-
concept and a commercial/legal framework before architectural commitment.
12.2 Real-Time Collaborative Playlist Integration
Vibe Atlas's current architecture treats playlists as static metadata (content appears 
in user playlists; playlist inclusion is one input to the Community heuristic). A real-
time collaborative playlist model — where multiple users can co-edit a playlist 
simultaneously, and where collaborative listening sessions are visible to other users 
— would significantly enrich the social layer of the platform. Key design questions: 
How does collaborative listening affect session context signals for the 
personalization model? How do we render live collaborative activity in the grid 
without disrupting individual discovery flow? How do we handle conflicts in 
simultaneous playlist edits?


===== PAGE 59 =====
12.3 Generative AI Vibe Matching (Natural 
Language → Vibe Vector)
The current Vibe Taxonomy is a 120-node fixed hierarchy. A generative AI vibe 
matching system would allow users to describe what they want in completely 
freeform natural language ("music that sounds like driving through the desert at 
dusk"), which would be transformed into a continuous vibe vector in the embedding 
space rather than a discrete taxonomy node selection. This would replace the 
current Semantic search mode's crude approximation of mood-based queries with a 
purpose-built natural language interface.
The open question is evaluation: how do we measure whether a generative vibe 
vector accurately captures user intent, given the subjective nature of mood and 
setting descriptions? A human evaluation framework for vibe matching quality 
would need to be designed before this feature could graduate from research to 
production.
12.4 Creator-Owned Quality Signal Contributions
An experimental direction in which creators contribute verified signal attestations 
directly to the Quality Score pipeline — for example, live concert attendance data 
from a ticketing partner, merchandise sales data from a storefront integration, or 
verified radio airplay counts from a performing rights organization. The architectural 
challenge is integrating heterogeneous, creator-controlled signal sources while 
maintaining the integrity of the scoring system against manipulation via false 
attestations.
12.5 Multi-Modal Content Support (Video, 
Podcasts, Live Streams)
Vibe Atlas is currently designed for music and audio-primary content. Extension to 
video content (music videos, visual albums, live concert films), podcast content 
(long-form audio without a traditional track structure), and live streams (ephemeral 
content with fundamentally different engagement patterns) would require 
significant changes to the scoring model. Completion Rate as a retention signal 
behaves differently for a 90-minute live stream than for a 3-minute track; the 
Freshness decay model has no natural equivalent for ephemeral live content; and 


===== PAGE 60 =====
the grid card anatomy was designed around square album artwork, not landscape 
video thumbnails.
Multi-modal support is the most significant future architecture work item and would 
effectively constitute a new major version of the scoring and classification systems. 
It is deferred pending a product decision on content type expansion strategy.
Document Status and Review Process
This document is Version 1.0 and represents the architectural design as of the Phase 0 
kickoff. It will be updated at each phase gate with decisions made during 
implementation that diverge from the designs specified here. All changes require review 
and approval by the Engineering Lead and Product Lead. Significant architectural 
changes — defined as changes that affect inter-system contracts (API schemas, Kafka 
topic schemas, scoring weights, or classification algorithm logic) — additionally require 
sign-off from the Head of Platform Engineering. The document change history is 
maintained in the engineering wiki alongside this document.
